The best way to work through this

The failure mode for a roadmap like this is not running out of time. It is finishing twelve weeks of videos with nothing you can show anybody. So the unit of progress here is not a topic, it is a week that closed — and a week only closes when its build runs, you have answered the checkpoint out loud, and you have written the note.

Roughly a quarter of your time is learning and three quarters is building. If a week produced only notes, it did not happen; carry the build forward and cut the reading, never the other way round.

The weeks are ordered as dependencies, not preferences. Week 6 assumes you built the loop by hand in Week 5. Week 10 assumes there is something worth evaluating. Skipping forward is the one change that reliably breaks the plan.

MonLearn. Read and watch, take rough notes, write nothing polished.
TueExperiment. Smallest possible script that proves you understood Monday.
Wed–ThuBuild. The named project, in the open, badly at first.
FriBreak it. Failure modes, edge cases, the design you now regret.
SatHarden. Tests, README, diagram, numbers.
SunClose. Checkpoint out loud, write the note, set next week current.

At twelve to eighteen hours a week that shape fits. At eight, run each of these weeks over two calendar weeks rather than dropping the build — twenty-two half-speed weeks beats eleven abandoned ones.

Every week has a Files panel next to its title. Put the repository or Colab link in first — that is the thing that travels and the thing an interviewer opens. Then attach the notebook itself, or drop the whole project folder in, and the week keeps its own evidence. Attachments are held in this browser and a folder comes back out as a single .zip; the link is the durable copy.

Six rules

  • Ship, don't studyEvery week leaves behind working code, a diagram, an evaluation result or a production decision. Notes alone do not count as a closed week.
  • Architecture firstBuild the agent loop by hand before LangGraph. Call the API directly before a framework. You cannot debug what you never assembled.
  • Measure before claiming“It works on my examples” is not a result. From Week 4 onward every claim about quality needs a number behind it.
  • Three-line notesWhat clicked, what broke, what to revisit. Written the day it happened. An essay on Sunday is a worse record than three lines on Wednesday.
  • Timebox the stuckNinety minutes stuck: write the question down, give it one more hour, then move on and log it. Come back when the next week has taught you the missing piece.
  • Depth over coverageOne system you can defend end to end beats six tutorials. When time runs short, deepen the capstone rather than adding a project.

What goes wrong

  • The tutorial loop. Finishing courses feels like progress and produces nothing an interviewer can look at. Courses are for unblocking a build, not for completing.
  • Framework first. LangGraph before you can write the loop gives you a system you can run and cannot fix.
  • “Looks good” evaluation. No dataset, no number, no idea whether last week's prompt change helped or hurt.
  • Demo-grade repositories. No README, no failure modes, no cost figures. The work existed; the evidence does not.
  • Multi-agent for its own sake. Three agents where one would do, defended by nothing but the fact that it was harder to build.
Phase 1

AI Engineering

Weeks 1–12

The goal is not to become a traditional ML engineer. It is to become a software engineer who can design, build, evaluate and operate production AI and agentic systems — with your existing Java, Spring and system-design experience kept, not replaced. Each week names one project; the projects compound, and by Week 12 they are one system.

01

ML Foundations

0/12

Learn the minimum ML vocabulary needed to read modern AI systems work. You are not becoming an ML researcher; you are becoming fluent enough that nobody can bluff you.

Learn

  • AI vs ML vs deep learning; supervised vs unsupervised; what “learning” actually means
  • Features, labels, and the train / validation / test split — why the test set is touched once
  • Regression vs classification, and the metrics that go with each (RMSE and MAE vs precision, recall, F1)
  • Overfitting, underfitting and the bias–variance trade-off
  • Cross-validation, and why evaluation data must never leak into training
  • Baselines: why “predict the mean” or “predict the majority class” is the number to beat

Build — ML Prediction Notebook

  • Pick one small tabular dataset; write down what a single row means before you model anything
  • Establish a dumb baseline and record its score — this is your floor
  • Preprocess: missing values, categorical encoding, scaling where the model needs it
  • Train two models and compare them on held-out data, never on training data
  • Error analysis: pull the twenty worst predictions and write a paragraph on why they fail
Ship gate — the week is not done until this is true

The notebook runs top to bottom on a fresh kernel, and the README states the baseline, the final metric, and one sentence on what the model still gets wrong.

Interview checkpoint — answer it out loud, from memory

Explain training vs testing, overfitting, and how you would know your model is any good — without writing a single formula.

The trap this week is completeness. Do not implement every classical algorithm. Stop the moment the end-to-end lifecycle is clear and move on.

    02

    Deep Learning and Transformers

    0/12

    Build working intuition for the machinery under an LLM, so that “context window”, “embedding” and “attention” stop being magic words you repeat.

    Learn

    • Neurons, weights, loss, gradient descent and backpropagation — the loop, not the calculus
    • Tokenisation: why text becomes subword units, and what that costs you in practice
    • Embeddings as geometry: similarity, distance, and what “semantic” actually means here
    • Attention and self-attention: query, key, value — and why it displaced recurrence
    • The transformer block: multi-head attention, feed-forward, residual connections, layer norm
    • Encoder vs decoder vs encoder–decoder; autoregressive generation, temperature and top-p

    Build — Embedding Explorer

    • Tokenise a paragraph and print the tokens; find a string that tokenises far worse than it looks
    • Embed a corpus of 100–200 short texts with an off-the-shelf model
    • Compute cosine similarity and retrieve nearest neighbours for ten queries
    • Find and record one case where “similar vectors” are not “similar meanings”
    • Project to 2-D, plot it, and write down what the clusters actually are
    Ship gate — the week is not done until this is true

    You can point at any retrieved neighbour and explain, in embedding terms, why it came back.

    Interview checkpoint — answer it out loud, from memory

    What does self-attention compute, and why do transformers handle long-range dependencies better than RNNs?

    This is the one week where theory earns its keep. Level 9 of the Syllabus (Inside the Model) is the long-form version of exactly this material — use it if a concept will not land.

      03

      LLM Engineering

      0/12

      Treat the LLM as what it is: an unreliable remote service sitting inside an otherwise normal software system.

      Learn

      • Prompt structure: system vs user vs assistant, few-shot, and what actually moves the output
      • Structured outputs and JSON schemas; validating model output like any untrusted input
      • Function and tool calling: the contract, the schema, and the error path
      • Streaming, latency budgets, token accounting and cost per request
      • Model selection: when a small model is enough, and when it is not
      • Temperature, determinism, and why “it worked yesterday” is not a defence

      Build — Expense Analyzer API

      • Endpoint takes free-form expense text and returns a validated object: amount, currency, category, date
      • Enforce the schema at the boundary — reject and retry on a schema violation, never pass it through
      • Handle refusal, truncation, malformed JSON, timeout and rate limit as distinct cases
      • Log tokens in and out plus cost per request; print a running daily total
      • Write twenty adversarial inputs (ambiguous, multi-currency, no amount) and record the behaviour of each
      Ship gate — the week is not done until this is true

      The API never returns an unvalidated model response, and every failure mode above has a test that proves it.

      Interview checkpoint — answer it out loud, from memory

      What is tool calling, and why should LLM output be validated exactly like an external API response?

      Keep this version framework-light. You are learning the raw mechanics; a framework here would hide the exact failures you need to see.

        04

        RAG

        0/12

        Build a retrieval pipeline and learn the lesson that decides most RAG projects: retrieval quality, not model quality, caps answer quality.

        Learn

        • Document parsing — PDFs, HTML, and the information lost at this very first step
        • Chunking strategies, overlap, and how chunk boundaries silently destroy answers
        • Metadata and filtering; hybrid search combining dense vectors with BM25
        • Top-k and reranking, and the recall against precision trade you are making
        • Grounding and citation: making the answer point at the passage it came from
        • Retrieval failure vs generation failure — and how to tell which one you have

        Build — Personal Knowledge RAG

        • Ingest your own notes and PDFs; store every chunk with its source metadata
        • Answer with inline citations that resolve to the exact passage
        • Add a floor: when top-k similarity falls below a threshold, answer “not in the corpus”
        • Write twenty test questions with a known answer and a known source document
        • Measure retrieval hit-rate as a separate number from answer quality
        Ship gate — the week is not done until this is true

        You can state your retrieval hit-rate as a number, and the system refuses to answer when retrieval is weak instead of inventing something.

        Interview checkpoint — answer it out loud, from memory

        Why does RAG fail even when the LLM is strong? Separate retrieval failure from generation failure in your answer.

        The twenty test questions are not optional busywork. Without them you cannot tell a chunking change from a prompt change, and every later week depends on that skill.

          05

          Agents Without Frameworks

          0/12

          Understand the agent loop completely before any framework gets to hide it from you.

          Learn

          • The loop: model proposes → tool executes → result returns → model continues
          • Tool selection, and how much of it is really driven by the tool description you wrote
          • State: what carries between turns and what gets re-derived each time
          • Termination: step budgets, success conditions, and the infinite-loop failure
          • Retries, timeouts and partial failure inside a tool call
          • Workflow vs agent — when the flow is known, do not let a model decide it

          Build — Tool-Using Assistant

          • Implement the loop by hand; no agent framework this week
          • Three tools: calculator, search (real or mocked) and document lookup
          • An explicit step budget and an explicit termination condition
          • Retry with backoff on tool error, and surface the error back to the model as a result
          • Log every step: tool chosen, arguments, result, and the reasoning that led there
          Ship gate — the week is not done until this is true

          You can read the step log of any run and explain every decision the agent made, without guessing.

          Interview checkpoint — answer it out loud, from memory

          Walk through one complete agent loop end to end — tool selection, result handling, termination.

          This is the highest-leverage week in the phase. Engineers who skip it can use LangGraph but cannot debug it.

            06

            LangGraph and Stateful Orchestration

            0/12

            Move from a hand-rolled loop to state that is explicit, inspectable and resumable.

            Learn

            • State schema; nodes and edges as a graph rather than a script
            • Conditional routing — and why it is testable in a way a prompt never is
            • Tool nodes and how they differ from a plain function call
            • Persistence and checkpoints
            • Interrupts and human-in-the-loop
            • Replay and time-travel debugging of a previous run

            Build — Stateful Research Agent

            • Graph: plan → retrieve → analyse → verify → answer
            • Persist state to a checkpoint store, not to memory
            • Add an interrupt before the final answer for human approval
            • Kill the process mid-run, restart it, and resume from the checkpoint
            • Add a verify node that can route back to retrieve when evidence is thin
            Ship gate — the week is not done until this is true

            You can stop the agent halfway, restart the process, and resume without losing any work.

            Interview checkpoint — answer it out loud, from memory

            When is a graph or workflow preferable to an autonomous agent? Explain state and conditional routing.

            Port the Week 5 agent rather than starting fresh. The diff between your loop and the graph is the actual lesson.

              07

              MCP

              0/12

              Learn the standard boundary between an agent and the systems it is allowed to touch.

              Learn

              • MCP client vs server, and what responsibility lives on each side
              • Tools, resources and prompts — three different primitives, not three names for one

                08

                Multi-Agent Systems

                0/12

                Use more than one agent only where specialisation or delegation genuinely pays for the cost and latency it adds.

                Learn

                • Supervisor and manager patterns
                • Agent-as-tool vs handoff — the difference in control flow and in what context travels
                • Planner → executor → reviewer
                • Shared state, and the context that must move with a handoff
                • How cost and latency multiply with every additional agent
                • When not to use multi-agent — which, honestly, is most of the time

                Build — Software Engineering Agent

                • Requirement in → planner → implementer → reviewer
                • The reviewer can reject work and send it back, under a hard revision limit
                • One shared state object; a step and token budget for the whole run, not per agent
                • Run the same ten tasks through a single-agent baseline
                • Record cost and latency for both and put them side by side
                Ship gate — the week is not done until this is true

                You have a number showing whether multi-agent actually beat the single agent — and you believe the number.

                Interview checkpoint — answer it out loud, from memory

                Compare supervisor and agents-as-tools with handoffs. When would you use each?

                If the baseline wins, that is a result, not a failed week. Say so in the README — it is a better interview story than a system you cannot justify.

                  09

                  Agent Harness and Reliability

                  0/12

                  Build the engineering layer that makes an agent safe to leave running unattended.

                  Learn

                  • Permissions and tool policies — allow-lists, not deny-lists
                  • Retries, timeouts, circuit breaking and termination conditions
                  • Context management: compaction, summarisation, and deciding what to drop
                  • Memory: session vs durable, and what should never be remembered at all
                  • Guardrails on input and on output
                  • Human approval gates on irreversible actions; idempotency of every tool

                  Build — Production Agent Harness

                  • Wrap the Week 6 agent with a permission gate, retry policy, timeout and step budget
                  • Persist state; make each tool idempotent or explicitly mark it not-retryable
                  • Require human approval before any write or side-effecting tool call
                  • Structured logs with one trace id per run, threaded through every step
                  • Document every failure mode you handle — and every one you knowingly do not
                  Ship gate — the week is not done until this is true

                  A written failure-mode table exists, and the agent cannot spend unbounded money or take an irreversible action while unattended.

                  Interview checkpoint — answer it out loud, from memory

                  How do you stop an agent looping, exceeding its budget, or calling a dangerous tool?

                  The failure-mode table is the deliverable people remember. It is the clearest signal that you have operated a system rather than demoed one.

                    10

                    Evaluation and Observability

                    0/12

                    Replace “looks good to me” with a number you would be willing to defend in a review.

                    Learn

                    • Building an eval set: where the cases come from and how many you actually need
                    • Deterministic graders vs LLM-as-judge — and the judge's own biases
                    • Correctness, groundedness, tool-selection accuracy and trajectory evaluation
                    • Regression testing: the suite that must pass before a prompt change ships
                    • Traces and spans — what is worth recording on every run

                    Build — Agent Evaluation Suite

                    • One hundred cases: input, expected answer, expected tools, expected sources

                    • Wire tracing so that clicking a failed case opens its full trace

                              15

                              State and Component Architecture

                              0/12

                              Decide where state lives. Most React codebases are difficult for exactly one reason: they got this wrong.

                              Learn

                              • State ownership and lifting state to the nearest common parent
                              • Derived state — never store what you can compute
                              • useState vs useReducer, and the point at which the switch pays off
                              • useContext and its real re-render cost
                              • Custom hooks: extracting behaviour rather than markup
                              • Purity, and why a component must not lie about its inputs

                              Build — Kanban Board

                              • A task board with columns, driven by a single reducer
                              • Move tasks between columns; support undo through the reducer
                              • Extract at least two custom hooks with names that describe behaviour
                              • Use Context only where prop drilling is genuinely painful, and say why in a comment
                              • Persist and rehydrate board state without breaking on a schema change
                              Ship gate — the week is not done until this is true

                              No piece of state is derivable from another, and one reducer owns every board mutation.

                              Interview checkpoint — answer it out loud, from memory

                              When do you reach for useReducer over useState, and why is Context not a state manager?

                                              Interview standard

                                              By the end you should be able to take any of these and talk for five minutes from your own code rather than from a blog post you read.

                                              • Workflow vs agent vs multi-agent, and when each is the wrong choice.
                                              • Where state lives in your system, and why it lives there.
                                              • RAG failure modes, and how you tell retrieval failure from generation failure.
                                              • MCP, the protocol boundary, and where authorisation belongs.
                                              • The agent harness as a reliability boundary: permissions, retries, budgets, approval.
                                              • Evaluation and regression testing — how you know a change helped.
                                              • Cost per request, and what you did to bring it down.
                                              • React state vs server state, and why fetch-in-effect goes wrong.
                                              • Why unnecessary effects are harmful, with an example from your own code.
                                              • Caching, retries, idempotency and rate limiting across the whole stack.
                                              • Prompt injection through tool output, and what you did about it.
                                              • How you would design the system at ten times the scale.

                                              Project standard

                                              Every project on this page is finished when all eight of these exist. A repository missing them is a draft, however good the code is.

                                              • README that states what it does and what it does not.
                                              • Architecture diagram, even if hand-drawn and photographed.
                                              • Setup instructions that work from a clean clone.
                                              • Tests, and one regression they actually caught.
                                              • Sample inputs and outputs.
                                              • Known limitations, written honestly.
                                              • Screenshots or a short recording.
                                              • A closing section: what I would change at 10× scale.
